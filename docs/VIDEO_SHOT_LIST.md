# VIDEO_SHOT_LIST: 2 minutes, one take

The brief's own structure is 15 s problem, 60 s demo, 30 s how it works, 15 s next. The demo carries half the video, so do not narrate the widgets.

## 0:00-0:15 problem
"We live next to Loudoun County, the biggest concentration of data centres in the world. At 7 PM the grid runs different power plants than at 2 AM, so the same EV charge can emit very different CO2. Nobody tells a family or an operator when the cleanest hours are. Clean-Hour does."

## 0:15-1:15 demo (app already open on Virginia)
1. Choose "Charge my electric car", "Up to 24 hours". Read the big answer out loud with whatever number the app shows: "Start ... at ..., about X% cleaner than starting at midnight."
2. Point at the green-to-red strip and the outlined window: "That bar is the next 72 hours; greener is cleaner."
3. Open "What actually happened": "Following the advice cut CO2 by X%; the best possible was Y%."
4. Switch to "up to 3 days": "More slack, more savings, and here the forecast clearly beats the simple rules."
5. California for four seconds: "In sunny California a plain run-at-midday rule is almost as good." Then Britain: "In wind-driven Britain the forecast matters, but the official NESO forecast beats ours, and we say so."

## 1:15-1:45 how it works
"Public grid data, January 2023 to November 2026: PJM fuel mix, weather forecasts, demand. We train on 2023-2025 and test on every day of 2026, never shuffled, against four baselines. Our first model was worse than repeat-yesterday in California, 44 grams against 35, so we changed the target and it beat it. Then we found the thing we did not expect: a better forecast was not a better choice of hours."

## 1:45-2:00 limit and next
"This is the grid average, not the marginal emissions of the plant that actually ramps up, and we flag that everywhere. Next: a real marginal signal, and one real family plus one data-centre operator. Takeaway: shifting saves emissions, but a better forecast is not automatically a better choice."
